# Choosing a mesh for your Palace simulation

If you're not sure how fine to mesh your gds2palace model, or whether adaptive mesh refinement (AMR) is worth turning on, this page summarizes what we found across four mesh convergence studies on real IHP SG13G2 structures: a spiral inductor, a transformer, a D-band coupled-line balun, and a 2:1 edge-coupled balun. Each study reruns the same layout at several mesh settings and compares the results, so you can see directly what accuracy you get for what runtime/memory cost.

## Quick recommendations

- **Start with a mesh cell size that gives you at least 2-3 cells across your narrowest trace or gap.** The structures here have trace widths and gaps from 2-8 µm, and a cell size resolving that gap with 2-3 cells consistently gave results within a percent or two of the finest mesh tried, at a fraction of the runtime. Going coarser than the gap itself is the worst offender — one study's 5 µm mesh, wider than its own 2 µm coupled-line gap, was clearly the least accurate point in the entire set (and a different study's coarsest point crashed the solver outright).
- **Use order 2 (Palace's default) for any result you plan to report or trust.** Order 1 runs several times faster, but gave up accuracy in every study — in one case a fairly constant offset regardless of mesh refinement, in another a frequency/depth shift that shrank (but didn't fully close) as the mesh refined. Either way, order 1 is fine for a quick rough look (e.g. checking your port setup or layout), not for final numbers — and don't assume a finer order-1 mesh reliably closes the gap to order 2.
- **Don't assume AMR will "just work" if you give it enough iterations.** In every study that let it run more than 2 iterations, AMR kept refining well past the point where the answer had already stopped changing, burning much more time and memory for no real accuracy gain — and even after using its entire iteration budget, it still hadn't satisfied its own convergence target. Capped at 2 iterations, results were mixed: sometimes it beat a directly-run uniform mesh of comparable accuracy, sometimes a plain uniform mesh was just as good for less cost. Either way, cap it around 2 iterations and compare against a uniform mesh before trusting it blindly.
- **When in doubt, run two mesh sizes and compare.** That's really all a convergence study is — if your result doesn't change much between a medium and a fine mesh, you're probably fine; if it does, go finer.

## The four studies

- **[Spiral inductor](mesh_convergence_inductor/mesh_convergence_report.md)** — a simple 2-port coil. Inductance and Q factor were evaluated directly, alongside S-parameters. This is the easiest case: even a fairly coarse mesh gets inductance right to within about 1%, and it's a study that also compares FEM order 1 vs. order 2.  
Note this is a single-ended inductor, where current in neighboring turns flows in a similar direction. This means that the local field between adjacent traces changes less sharply than in a differential (symmetric) inductor, where neighboring traces of the two half-coils carry opposite-phase current. That likely makes this structure gentler on the mesh than a differential inductor of similar dimensions would be.
- **[Transformer](mesh_convergence_transformer/mesh_convergence_report.md)** — a 5-port coupled-coil transformer with a center tap. Covers mixed-mode S-parameters and the real input impedance seen under a floating differential load.
- **[D-band balun](mesh_convergence_D-band_balun/mesh_convergence_report.md)** — a compact edge-coupled-line balun for 140-170 GHz. The smallest, most tightly-coupled geometry of the four, and the one where mesh choice mattered most.
- **[2:1 edge-coupled balun](mesh_convergence_balun2x1/mesh_convergence_report.md)** — a single octagonal loop of 4 edge-coupled lines (8 µm width, 2 µm gap) giving a 2:1 primary:secondary turns ratio, no center tap. Also compares order 1 vs. order 2; unlike the inductor study, the order-1 error here shrinks as the mesh refines rather than staying roughly constant — a reminder that this trade-off is structure-dependent.

## Bottom line

These are four different structures at four different frequency ranges, so the exact numbers won't transfer directly to your design — but the pattern was consistent across all of them: a moderately fine uniform mesh (order 2) got close to the best achievable answer quickly and predictably, while chasing AMR's default convergence target or dropping to order 1 for speed both came with real trade-offs, and the size of those trade-offs varied by structure. If your structure is broadly similar in scale to one of these four, start from that study's recommended mesh settings and verify with one finer run before trusting the result.

## Norm & Max: the dirty details of Palace's error indicator

*If you're coming from HFSS or CST: `error-indicators.csv`'s "Norm" and "Maximum" are not a mesh quality score and not that "Delta S" convergence criterion you're used to. It's a genuinely different kind of number, worth understanding before you read anything into it — good or bad.*

**What they actually are.** Palace computes one value per mesh element: a Zienkiewicz–Zhu-style residual comparing the raw FEM E/B field on that element to a smoothed reconstruction of it, scaled by the total EM field energy in the domain at that frequency — so it's a *relative*, energy-normalized local error, not an absolute field error, and it has nothing to do with S-parameters or any other output quantity. "Norm" is the global L2 norm of that per-element vector across the whole mesh; "Maximum" is the single worst element in it.

**Where frequency enters.** Every frequency point Palace actually runs a full 3D solve at — every point in a plain sweep, or only the handful of greedily-picked points in an adaptive-PROM sweep (the "Greedy iteration ... n=NN" lines in the solver log) — contributes its own per-element error. These don't get summed or maxed across frequency; they're combined by a running **root-mean-square** average. So the Norm/Max you see is an RMS blend over however many frequency samples got solved — and for an adaptive sweep, that count isn't fixed across mesh variants, since it depends on a *different* tolerance (the frequency sweep's own convergence target, not the mesh-refinement one).

**Why Max often drops faster than Norm as the mesh refines.** Max tracks one specific worst element — usually a real, fixable culprit (a coarse element straddling a thin gap, a sharp trace corner) — so refining the mesh there shrinks it directly. Norm is a global aggregate; sharp conductor edges and corners are classic field singularities that converge slowly under plain uniform refinement, so their relative contribution to the global sum keeps showing up no matter how fine the overall mesh gets. Palace's own documentation says as much: its AMR tolerance "is not a strict relative-error bound for every derived quantity such as S-parameters" — exactly why every study on this page computes `Max|ΔS|` directly from the S-parameters instead of trusting this indicator to say when to stop.

**Bottom line:** a small, or a stubbornly plateaued, Norm isn't good or bad news about your actual result by itself — it's measuring something else entirely. Trust the delta-S tables in these reports, not the solver's internal error indicator.
